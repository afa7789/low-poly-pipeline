Clean 2D model-sheet blockout on one solid light-grey background (#CCCCCC), drawn as coarse flat design art — a rough flat-colour blockout, not a photo, not a 3D render, not a polished illustration. Five orthographic drawings of the same unit — a Mech Assault Tank (id h099) from a dieselpunk dark-fantasy low-poly game — one drawing per panel in a five-panel layout: a top row of three panels (front, left profile, back) and a bottom row of two panels (top-down, bottom-up), with wide empty margins between them. Exactly five drawings: never a second profile view.

The subject: a short, fat, stubby cartoon tank, a squat toy-like brick on four huge wheels, chunky and cartoonish like a Warcraft 3 vehicle. This is the Light Tank from the reference sheet, the same character upgraded: bigger, wider, heavier and better armed, but NOT longer in proportion. It must look compressed, as if the old long tank were squashed front to back.

Hard proportions, the most important rule of this sheet:
- In profile the whole tank, plough tip to rear plate, is only about 1.2 times as long as it is tall, wheels to turret top. It must fit nearly in a square.
- Seen from above, the hull is almost as wide as it is long.
- Only four wheels, two per side, never six. They are oversized: each wheel is more than half the height of the hull, and the two wheels on a side almost touch, with no gap of hull between them.
- The plough is short and tucked tight against the nose, sticking out only a little.
- The turret is big and fat, nearly as long as the hull roof.
- The cannon is a short stub: its muzzle ends above the plough tip, never past it.
All corners are rounded and swollen, all shapes thick and simple, like a toy. Few large shapes, no thin parts.

What stays from the Light Tank: the curved grey plough with a thick brass rim on the nose, the four wide grey wheels with brass rims and dark spoked brass hubs, the boxy brass-framed hull with a dark charcoal deck, the large red flank panel, the flat brass-framed grey rear plate, the ribbed copper block on each shoulder, and the two fat exhaust stacks with black tips splayed in a V at the rear.

What grows: everything is thicker and heavier. The plough becomes a wider, massive wedge with a blunt central ridge. The small mortar is replaced by a fat round grey drum turret between two thick brass rings, carrying one short thick brass cannon that points straight forward and ends in a flared hexagonal muzzle with a dark bore. The copper shoulder blocks become fatter and shorter. The exhaust stacks become much thicker and shorter.

Every panel holds exactly one full-body figure, never cropped, never touching its neighbour. The five figures are the same unit: same proportions, same silhouette, same props in the same places, same scale, same ground line. Neutral static pose, a wheeled vehicle with no head, torso, arms or legs: hull level, all four wheels straight and on the ground, cannon level and pointing forward, no recoil, no smoke, no muzzle flash. Squared-on orthographic projection, no perspective, no foreshortening. Drawn with a heavy coarse line over a few large flat blocks, with hard clean edges and no small hardware: no rivet rows, no chains, no hoses, no gear teeth, no panel lines, one flat tone per surface, no texture, no grunge, no gradients, no gloss, no cast shadows.
